[G.017] 1. Why Singapore
[G.018] Singapore ranked
[G.019] 1st in math
[G.020] in two of the leading international assessments: in
[G.021] TIMSS 2023
[G.022] , with 4th and 8th graders (615 and 605 points, on a scale centered at 500), and in
[G.023] PISA 2022
[G.024] , with 15-year-olds (575 points; the OECD average is 472).
[G.025] [Sources: TIMSS & PIRLS International Study Center / IEA, TIMSS 2023 International Results, 2024; OECD, PISA 2022 Results, Volume I, 2023.]
[G.026] The foundation of the method isn't new: the idea of going from concrete to drawing and only then to calculation comes from psychologist Jerome Bruner (1966), and bar drawing entered Singapore's primary school curriculum in 1983.
[G.027] [Source: Ministry of Education, Singapore,
[G.028] The Singapore Model Method for Learning Mathematics
[G.029] , 2009.]
[G.030] What stands out isn't the amount of calculation. It's
[G.031] how
[G.032] children there learn to solve word problems: before calculating, they
[G.033] draw the problem
[G.034] as a bar. The drawing shows which operation to use.
[G.035] The problem this method solves
[G.036] Many children know how to do the math but get stuck when it comes inside a story. They look at the numbers and guess: “Do I add?” “Do I multiply?” The problem isn't a lack of times tables. It's that no one taught them to
[G.037] see
[G.038] what the story is asking.
[G.039] The bar solves this. When your child draws what's happening, the operation shows up on its own: if a piece is missing, it's subtraction; if the pieces are equal, it's multiplication or division.
[G.040] You don't need to know how to teach math.
[G.041] Your role is to ask the right questions (they're ready-made in this guide) and ask for the drawing. Each mission comes with the answer key, with the expected drawing and the explanation of each stage.
[G.042] 2. The 3 steps: Concrete → Drawing → Calculation (the “abstract” step)
[G.043] The Singapore method introduces each new topic in 3 steps, in this order. In the missions, the stage marked
[G.044] “draw the problem”
[G.045] is where your child does all three.
[G.046] Concrete
[G.047] Hands-on.
[G.048] Your child solves it with objects: beans, bottle caps, paper strips. If the story has 24 stickers, they set aside 24 beans and take away the ones that were given away.
[G.049] Your part:
[G.050] set out the objects and let your child handle them.
[G.051] Skip it when:
[G.052] they can already solve it without touching the objects.
[G.053] Drawing
[G.054] With a bar.
[G.055] Instead of the beans, they draw a rectangle that stands for the quantity and split it into parts. They put a
[G.056] on the piece they want to find out.
[G.057] ask “Show me that in a bar” and wait.
[G.058] Move on when:
[G.059] they explain the drawing in their own words.
[G.060] Calculation
[G.061] With numbers.
[G.062] Looking at the drawing, they write the equation and solve it. Then they go back to the drawing to check that the answer makes sense.
[G.063] ask “What operation is the drawing asking for?”
[G.064] Sign of mastery:
[G.065] they make the drawing quickly, on their own, and get the math right.
[G.066] 3. The bar (the “bar model”): 3 drawings that solve almost everything
[G.067] Almost every problem in the missions, from Level 1 to Level 3, fits one of these 3 drawings. The bar doesn't need to be pretty or measured with a ruler: the bigger part just needs to look bigger.
[G.068] A. Part-whole (putting together and taking away)
[G.069] “Ana had 24 stickers and gave 15 to her sister. How many does she have left?”
[G.070] 24 stickers
[G.071] 15 given
[G.072] The whole bar is the whole (24). One piece is what she gave away (15). The other is the
[G.073] . Knowing the whole and one part, it's subtraction: 24 − 15 = 9. If the
[G.074] is on top (on the whole), it's addition.
[G.075] B. Comparison (how many more, how many fewer)
[G.076] “Léo has 15 trading cards. Bia has 9. How many more does Léo have?”
[G.077] Léo
[G.078] Bia
[G.079] One bar for each person, lined up on the left. The dashed piece is the difference: what the shorter bar is missing to match the longer one. 15 − 9 = 6.
[G.080] C. Equal pieces (multiplying, dividing, and fractions)
[G.081] “There are 4 boxes with 6 pencils each. How many pencils in all?”
[G.082] 6 pencils
[G.083] Pieces of the same size. If the
[G.084] is on the total, it's multiplication (4 × 6 = 24). If the total is known and the
[G.085] is on a piece, it's division.
[G.086] Fractions: “The character's energy bar has 80 points and is 3/4 full. How many points does the character have?”
[G.087] 80 points
[G.088] Split the bar into 4 equal pieces (the “bottom” of the fraction) and shade 3 (the “top”). The answer is the value of the 3 shaded pieces together. Each piece is worth 80 ÷ 4 = 20; the 3 shaded ones are worth 60.

[G.137] 6. Mastery before moving on
[G.138] The Singapore method works with mastery: you only move on when the current step is solid. At home, use this simple rule:
[G.139] Move on to the next mission when your child can explain the drawing from the “draw the problem” stage on their own.
[G.140] If not yet, redo just that stage another day, with the numbers changed (each mission includes a suggestion on the adult page). Repeating with new numbers isn't falling behind: it's what makes the method work.
[G.141] To change levels:
[G.142] the 10th mission of each level is the
[G.143] Final Mission
[G.144] , which mixes all the drawings from the level. Your child moves up to the next level when the code word works out and they can explain at least 3 of the 4 drawings on their own. Mark it on the Mastery Map.
[G.145] Getting the math right without being able to explain the drawing isn't mastery yet. Explaining the drawing and getting the math wrong from carelessness already is: just ask them to check.
[G.146] 7. The routine at home
[G.147] Frequency:
[G.148] 2 or 3 missions a week, 20 to 35 minutes each. Short and frequent is better than a weekend marathon.
[G.149] Before:
[G.150] read the mission's adult page (2 minutes) and set out the objects for the Concrete step.
[G.151] During:
[G.152] read the opening with an air of mystery, let your child decide the operations, and ask for the drawing at the marked stage.
[G.153] After:
[G.154] check the code word and ask: “Explain your drawing to me.”
[G.155] Siblings:
[G.156] print the mission once and have them switch roles at each stage: reader, illustrator, and number cruncher. If they're at different levels, each one does the mission for their level and, at the end, they tell each other how they drew it.
[G.157] Draw-It Card:
[G.158] print it and leave it on the table during the missions. It's a reminder of the steps and the 3 drawings.
[G.159] 8. The 3 levels and where to start
[G.160] The 30 missions are split into 3 difficulty levels, with 10 missions each. You choose where to start based on what your child can already do, not on age or grade.
[G.161] Level
[G.162] What's in it
[G.163] Start here if…
[G.164] 1 · The first drawing
[G.165] Putting together, taking away, comparing, equal groups, sharing, time, meters, and halves, thirds, and fourths. Numbers up to 100. The bar comes ready-made and your child just fills it in.
[G.166] they still ask “Do I add or subtract?”
[G.167] 2 · Two steps
[G.168] Problems with 3 parts, “times as many,” fractions of a quantity, division with remainders, kilograms and grams, liters, seconds, and graphs. Numbers up to 1,000. Your child splits the bar.
[G.169] they can already solve putting-together, taking-away, and comparing problems on their own.
[G.170] 3 · Multi-step
[G.171] Decimals, proportion (find 1, then the total), before and after, sum and difference, fraction of what's left, unequal parts, simple percentages, perimeter, and area. Numbers up to 10,000. Your child draws on their own.
[G.172] they can already solve “times as many” problems and fractions of a quantity.
[G.173] When in doubt, start with Level 1: the first missions are quick, and getting things right early on builds confidence. If it gets too easy, skip to that level's Final Mission. If they crack the code word and explain 3 of the 4 drawings, move up a level.
[G.174] 9. How to use the bonuses
[G.175] 30 Lightning Challenges:
[G.176] one 5-minute mini-problem a day, to draw before solving. Challenges 1 to 10 go with Level 1, 11 to 20 with Level 2, and 21 to 30 with Level 3. Use them on days without a mission: before dinner, in the car, in the waiting room.
[G.177] Mission Kit:
[G.178] give your child the agent badge before the first mission. Use one clue sheet per mission to write down the answers and the code word. The certificate comes when your child completes a level.
[G.179] Mastery Map:
[G.180] hang it on the fridge. Your child colors in the mission's space when the code word works out and they explain the drawing. The Final Mission is the gateway to the next level. At the bottom, there are 30 circles, one for each Lightning Challenge.
[G.181] stays on the table during the missions and challenges: Read, Draw, Solve, Check.
